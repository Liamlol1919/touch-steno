## Verdict: what most directly threatens a two-handed 0G chorded design

Rank by directness to our signal chain (tablet x/y at 91 Hz, both hands down, light touch, no travel, no tactile detent):

1. **(2) Whole-body/postural drift is the primary threat.** Our only cross-hand number (r=-0.86 at ~100 mm, removable by subtraction) is by hypothesis common-mode forearm/body motion. External anchors show ~3 deg / 30 s proprioceptive drift, ~10 deg conditioning-dependent bias collapsible to ~0 when slack/supported, 4-deg vs 10-deg orientation uncertainty on/off table, and mm-scale soft-tissue/postural shifts (3.5-5.9 mm nerve slide on 48 mm protraction). On a 224-mm pad with zero force threshold there is no mechanical ground truth; drift IS coordinate error unless common-mode is explicitly estimated and subtracted per frame. No source partitions this into hand vs finger mm — exactly our blind spot.
2. **(1) Bimanual coupling is second.** 55-ms symbolic-cue cost (vs 6 ms direct), synchrony tendency, and 113-ms selective-stop interference show the hands are not independent effectors when tasks differ. A two-hand chord inventory that requires asymmetric holds/releases will pay initiation and inhibition taxes no within-hand model captures.
3. **(5) Fatigue derates the ceiling third.** ~10 ms IKI inflation and +0.9 pp word-error over 100 min (≈5-11% throughput loss) means a peak events/sec ceiling cannot be quoted as sustained throughput.
4. **(3) Support trades speed for stability.** ~17-wpm penalty for wrist support and up to ~55-118% torque reductions with palm support frame the design knob: support the forearms/heels and recover the 4-deg regime, but expect slower alternation.
5. **(4) Two-hand chord advantage is unproven on error rate.** Speed parity takes ~25 sessions; the specific 1+1-across-hands vs 2-on-one-hand error comparison is NOT FOUND. Cannot claim two-hand chording is cleaner, only that it is learnable (~59 chords / 3 h; 160 vs 105 cpm at 35 h for novices).

## Measurement that would settle it on our hardware (single protocol, ~30 min)

Static + dynamic common-mode assay at 91 Hz, both hands resting (our exact posture). No new hardware:
1. **Static hold (10 min):** all contacts down, no voluntary motion, forearms resting as in sessions. Log raw x/y per contact. Compute per-frame common-mode (median dx, dy across all contacts) vs residuals. Report Allan deviation of common-mode and of differential (cross-hand pair 73->55/56 and within-hand pairs) in mm at 1/10/60/300 s; Pearson r and r^2 cross-hand vs within-hand; mm RMS before/after subtracting common-mode. Replicates the r=-0.86 finding and converts deg-scale literature to mm on our pad.
2. **Support toggle (2x5 min):** same hold with forearms fully supported vs deliberately floating (heels up). Same metrics + tremor-band (3-12 Hz) power. Tests the 4-deg vs 10-deg literature claim in mm and whether support changes cross-hand r.
3. **Alternating vs simultaneous taps (5 min):** paced 1-3 Hz alternate-hand single taps and synchronous two-hand taps. Report inter-hand interval SD, synchronization error, and whether one hand's timing predicts the other's (cross-correlation at lag 0/1). Tests 55-ms / synchrony / 113-ms claims with our sensor latency.
4. **Sustained chord bursts (10 min):** repeated two-hand chords at fixed metronome rate; track IKI drift (+ms) and mis-chord rate vs time. Tests the ~10 ms / +0.9 pp fatigue derate on 0G contacts.

Pass/fail for 0G design: if cross-hand common-mode RMS exceeds ~1/4 of minimum inter-target spacing after subtraction, or if alternate-hand jitter exceeds half the chord simultaneity window, the design must add per-frame de-drifting and wider simultaneity tolerance, not tighter thresholds.

## Agent-2 result: the body-drift explanation does NOT survive a proper test (2026-09-25 06:55)

W18 ranked whole-body/postural drift as the primary threat and attributed our cross-hand
r = −0.86 to common-mode forearm motion. That is testable, so it was tested. **It failed.**

Method: per frame, least-squares rigid fit `d_i = t + omega × r_i` over the **static**
contacts only (those below the motion gate), then subtract. The resting fingers are the
frame; that is what makes the fit well posed.

| pair | class | raw r | after rigid fit |
|---|---|---:|---:|
| 73 → 55 | cross-hand, ~100 mm | −0.858 | **−0.660** |
| 73 → 56 | cross-hand, ~100 mm | −0.848 | **−0.754** |
| 64 → 61 | long-finger neighbours | +0.958 | +0.953 |
| 56 → 57 | long-finger neighbours | +0.936 | +0.924 |
| 61 → 65 | long-finger neighbours | +0.893 | +0.888 |

A two-parameter rigid model removes only ~20 % of the cross-hand anti-correlation and leaves
the within-hand couplings untouched. So the cross-hand structure is **not** explained by
in-plane translation plus rotation.

Two traps found on the way, both worth recording:

- A first pass that fitted on **all** contacts reported −0.858 → **+0.602**, which looked like
  a clean confirmation. It was an artifact: when every contact moves the same way, the fit
  reads the gesture as a rotation and cancels the signal it is meant to measure. Anchors must
  be static contacts.
- The least-squares normal equations initially accumulated two numerators and returned half
  the true omega. Caught by a synthetic rotation test.

**What this changes.** W18's ranking loses its first item. The cross-hand anti-correlation
survives the simplest body-motion model, so it is either a more complex motion (out-of-plane,
elbow pivot outside the contact plane, forearm pronation) or a genuine bimanual interference
effect — which is W18's item 2, with its own numbers (55 ms symbolic-cue cost, 113 ms
selective-stop interference). That makes **bimanual interference, not drift, the top open
hardware question**, and it is a question the cued protocol can answer: alternating vs
simultaneous two-hand taps, per W18's protocol step 3.
